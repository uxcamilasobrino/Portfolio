Overview

Elara is a mobile web app for aesthetic clinics. During the consultation, it shows the patient a realistic AI simulation of the treatment on their own face, and it keeps every patient's photos as an organized, GDPR-conscious clinical record.

It started with field research. I visited Sunsolfigueres - Centre d'estètica and observed their processes on site. Two things stayed with me. First, patients almost always ask to see a final result in the first appointment, but the doctor can't show photos of other patients because of privacy. Second, the doctor kept every photo on her own phone and struggled to find a specific one when she needed it.

Elara answers both. The patient sees a credible preview on their own face, and every photo is stored per patient and per treatment, ready to find when needed.

Problem definition

In an aesthetic consultation, the outcome is explained with words. Patients can't picture how a treatment will look on them, and the usual aids (generic before and after galleries, sketches, photos of other patients) either don't look like them or can't be shown at all. The result is unrealistic expectations and hesitant decisions.

1

Patients can't picture the result

Words, sketches and generic galleries don't show their own face.

2

Doctors can't show real examples

Photos of other patients can't be shared for privacy reasons.

3

Clinical photos are hard to manage

They sit in a phone gallery, inconsistent and hard to search, and they count as health data under GDPR.

How might we

Show patients a credible result on their own face, while keeping every photo organized and private?

Goals

Primary goals
  • Let patients see a credible preview of their treatment, on their own face, during the consultation.
  • Give the doctor one place where every patient's photos are organized and easy to find.
Secondary goals
  • Capture consistent clinical photos: same pose, light and expression.
  • Protect patient data from day one: consent, EU hosting, automatic deletion.
  • Support the conversation without overpromising: results are labeled as estimates.

Key design decisions

DECISION 1

Guide the photo, don't trust it

The camera shows a face oval, checks that the phone is level and the light is even, and for follow-ups overlays the original photo as a translucent ghost.

Why: the simulation is only as good as the photo, and consistent photos are also the clinical record.

DECISION 2

One treatment, one photo type

Every treatment asks for the specific pose its effect needs, and the app only accepts the matching photo.

Why: in an early test, a raised eyebrow photo sent with a frown instruction made the AI change the patient's expression instead of the treated area.

DECISION 3

Trust built into the flow

Consent comes before any photo. A signature stays valid for 2 years, and the app explains when and why it asks again. The result is always labeled as an AI estimate, with no sharing and no marketing features.

Why: doctors and patients should never feel the tool is selling something.

DECISION 4

Less on screen, actions where they apply

I removed the home title, hint texts and menu descriptions, and moved each photo's actions onto the photo screen. Navigation follows the app hierarchy, including the iPhone swipe back gesture.

Why: the doctor is talking to a patient, so every screen has to be understood at a glance.

Decision 5: cut what isn't essential

The MVP focuses on the consultation. Offline saving was built and removed the same day because it added complexity and caused a bug. Without a connection, a full screen notice covers the app and nothing is lost.

Privacy and security

EU hosting

Servers, database and photo storage in Frankfurt. Photos are private, with no public links.

Consent first

No photo before consent, and the consent text is versioned.

Minimal traces

Logs never contain photos, names or notes. A separate access log records who viewed what.

Automatic deletion

Photos are deleted after 2 years, and a patient can be deleted completely, photos included.

Before real patients use it, the next steps are data processing agreements and a legal review.

Learning

This was my first project built entirely with Claude, and the first time I took a product all the way to production. Until now my work ended at the handoff. Here, decisions I would normally pass to engineers, like what happens offline, what gets deleted and when, or how an error is explained, became mine to make. I learned that designing with an AI means designing its limits as carefully as the interface, and that clear decisions and careful review matter more than speed. It also taught me when to cut: I built offline saving and removed it the same day.
